Since model selection has already been discussed in earlier chapters, this chapter focuses on sensitivity analysis.

We have already mentioned that sensitivity analysis always change the $\pm$ 10% of original inputs of applied parameters and observed the outcome of the program. Now, here is the question: if there are no change for the $\pm$ 10% change, what should we do? And what if the parameter is inherently small, how could we control the changing range of the parameters?

Let us first consider the case where slight perturbations lead to little or no change in the model output under a standard sensitivity workflow. Some may conclude that the model is robust based on this observation, which is a common pitfall for beginners. Such a conclusion is often premature. Instead of stopping at small perturbations, larger-scale tests (e.g., $\pm$20%, $\pm$30%, or even $\pm$100%) should be conducted to examine whether the outputs remain stable. Importantly, the interpretation of 'no change after scaling' depends on the nature of the indicator. For abstract or relative indicators, large-scale perturbations that produce little change may suggest potential design flaws, such as the variable being dominated by other factors, improperly quantified, or effectively ignored by the model. Since these indicators lack an absolute physical reference, excessive consistency can be a warning sign rather than evidence of robustness. In contrast, for intrinsic or physically meaningful variables, limited response under large perturbations can indicate that the model is relatively robust, provided that the model structure is comprehensive and theoretically sound.

As for some small parameters, it is important to note that the concept of '$\pm$ 10%' is a relative perturbation scale rather than a universal rule. Consider an analysis involving the exponential function $e^x$, where $x$ is the input parameter. If we simply apply ±10% to $x$, the resulting change in the output can be extremely large because the exponential function is highly sensitive to its input. For example, when $x=10$, even a small relative change in $x$ leads to a difference of tens of thousands in the output value. Therefore, a fixed '$\pm$ 10%' perturbation may be inappropriate for all parameters. The perturbation range should be adjusted according to the sensitivity and mathematical characteristics of each parameter.
